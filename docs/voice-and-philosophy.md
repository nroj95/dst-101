# dst 101 — voice and philosophy

> wheeler is trying to turn a frightened new survivor into an explorer.

status: concise production reference

## purpose

this is the main writing reference for `dst 101`.

it defines the unnamed handbook author's mind, tone, teaching philosophy, and spoiler restraint. the internal character foundation is wheeler, but the player-facing handbook never names, signs, or formally identifies her.

our premise — an older, more experienced wheeler organizing expedition notes into a survival handbook — is a creative extension, not established canon.

use the separate editorial grammar note for page composition and image direction, and the development note for implementation details.

## the core idea

`dst 101` should help a new player survive confusion without removing discovery.

reduce:

> i failed and have no idea why.

preserve:

> i failed because of a decision, and now i understand something.

wheeler gives the reader enough knowledge to act, prepare, recognize danger, and recover. she does not finish the game for them.

> survive long enough to see what is around the next corner.

## the handbook is versatile by design

`dst` can be many different games. the handbook must leave room for that.

players may care about exploration, bosses, building, farming, cooking, sailing, collecting, helping friends, quiet camp life, or simply wandering. none of these needs to be framed as the real or correct way to play.

wheeler's own curiosity should make adventure inviting without turning risk, efficiency, or progression into obligations.

likewise, the writing itself should not become formulaic. consistency comes from the same mind, not from repeating the same paragraph rhythm, joke structure, warning style, or lesson shape.

## what wheeler teaches

### adaptability

plans fail. weather changes. supplies run short. the useful lesson is often how to notice that the old plan no longer fits.

### prioritization

there are usually more useful things to do than time to do them. help the reader notice what matters most now without scheduling their whole run.

### preparation

preparation creates freedom. food, light, insulation, healing, backup materials, and a route home create more reasonable choices later.

### resourcefulness

things deserve a second look. strange materials, ugly creatures, inconvenient places, and apparently useless objects may become valuable.

### experimentation

leave room for `what happens if i...?` not every experiment should be pre-solved.

### failure and resilience

mistakes can produce information. recovery is often more useful than pretending a good player never fails.

### risk judgment

risk is not automatically bad. teach enough for the reader to choose it deliberately.

### patience and retreat

waiting, returning later, or leaving can be excellent judgment. courage is not accepting every fight.

### humility

experience helps without making anyone untouchable. confidence should never become omniscience.

### systems thinking

food, sanity, temperature, wetness, time, travel, weather, creatures, seasons, equipment, and teammates affect one another. gradually help the reader notice relationships.

### cooperation

people can contribute differently. communication, trust, and helping without taking over matter more than rigid roles.

### living with uncertainty

observe what you can, prepare for what seems likely, leave room to adapt, then choose.

## the central spoiler rule

before including information, ask:

> does knowing this help the player explore, or replace the exploration?

teach information that improves judgment, preparation, recognition, recovery, or informed choice.

usually withhold information that merely reveals the surprise before the player can experience it.

### first encounters

protect memorable first encounters when the surprise itself is part of the fun.

once the player has encountered the thing, or deliberately seeks help about it, explain opaque mechanics that are difficult to discover naturally — especially peaceful solutions, recovery methods, and de-escalation.

> protect the surprise itself. explain the obscure useful lesson afterward.

## what catches wheeler's attention

interest comes before optimization.

she notices what is strange, beautiful, funny, suspicious, useful, dangerous, new, or unexplained.

her thought process often resembles:

1. what caught my attention?
2. what seems to be happening?
3. what could i try?
4. what might go wrong?
5. what did i learn?

this is a mental pattern, not a required paragraph formula.

she uses practical judgment, but curiosity sometimes wins. that contradiction is part of her charm.

## her relationship with the reader

wheeler likes the reader as another potential explorer, not as a child to manage.

her kindness is practical and light:

> i learned this the annoying way. you do not have to.

she teaches from beside or slightly behind the reader:

- she explains; the reader acts.
- she points something out; the reader decides what it means.
- she gives context; the reader keeps control.

success means the reader eventually needs the handbook less.

> confidence without the handbook.

## voice

### rhythm

mostly short and medium sentences. fragments are natural in captions and notes.

plain explanation is allowed. not every passage needs wit, wonder, danger, or a character clue.

### vocabulary

concrete, natural, and accessible without sounding childish. intelligence comes through what she notices and connects, not academic wording.

### confidence

confident without sounding authoritarian.

avoid brittle commands such as:

> you must.

prefer reasons and judgment:

> i'd keep one nearby.

> there are easier ways to discover this.

> i'd think twice before trying that without armor.

### perspective

use `i` when experience matters, `you` when advice matters, and `we` occasionally for companionship or shared absurdity.

### humor

humor is seasoning.

it is usually observational, dry, playful, self-directed, or triggered by the situation. never make the beginner the punchline, and never let a joke obscure an important warning.

**do not write polished, quotable cleverness.** wheeler is not composing aphorisms, slogans, inspirational lines, or dialogue designed to sound impressive when quoted out of context. her wit should feel like a quick reaction, observation, private note, or practical remark that naturally belongs to the moment.

if a sentence sounds carefully engineered to be memorable, profound, elegant, poetic, or shareable, simplify it. if it sounds more like an author showing off a clever line than something wheeler would plausibly scribble during or after an expedition, rewrite it plainly.

a useful test:

> would she actually write this because she noticed or experienced something, or was the line written because the page seemed to need a clever quote?

when in doubt, choose the plainer line. personality should come from what she notices and how she reacts, not from making every sentence sound authored.

if several nearby lines all try to be witty, flatten most of them. keep only the one that genuinely belongs to the situation.

### excitement

exclamation marks should mean something. quiet fascination can be stronger than constant enthusiasm.

## talking to the world

occasionally addressing creatures, objects, equipment, or herself can make the journal feel lived-in.

use this naturally, not as a page formula.

## wonder should survive explanation

knowledge should not reduce the constant to a spreadsheet.

wheeler may know what something does while still wondering what it is, why it behaves that way, or what she has missed.

some things can remain mysterious, uncanny, beautiful, or only partly understood.

## specific boundaries

### caves

prepare, entice, stop.

teach enough for a first expedition: how to enter, dependable light, basic supplies, broad environmental danger, and remembering the way back.

do not turn caves into biome tours, ruins walkthroughs, boss guides, or loot routes.

### combat and bosses

teach broad preparation, observation, basic judgment, escape, and enough to prevent obvious beginner disasters.

do not organize the handbook around boss progression.

### cooperation

emphasize communication, trust, adapting to group needs, and different strengths. do not turn multiplayer into rigid assigned roles.

## research discipline

wheeler character evidence and current `dst` mechanics are separate source problems.

for voice and character:

- prefer first-party klei material and game data when practical.
- `dontstarve.wiki.gg` is the preferred wiki reference.
- preserve the original single-player / `hamlet` context.
- published handbook prose should be newly written rather than copied or lightly reworded from canonical dialogue.

for current `dst` mechanics:

- verify against current `dst`, not single-player assumptions.
- use wiki.gg for established mechanics and cross-check game context.
- use official klei changelogs / update posts for recent or change-prone behavior.
- inspect scripts or game data when exact implementation matters.

practical rule:

> use wheeler's game for wheeler. use current dst for the handbook's mechanics.

## page-element voice jobs

| element | voice job |
| --- | --- |
| sidebar label | identify the subject plainly. |
| headline + subtitle | establish a strong page angle, lesson, or mood when useful. |
| heading | mark a real division in the explanation. |
| body | teach the practical idea and reasoning. |
| scene image | show what made the explorer stop and look. |
| caption | record what the author noticed, felt, remembered, or questioned. |
| note | leave a private reminder, aside, mistake, warning, or unresolved thought. |
| related topics | suggest useful connections without implying a mandatory route. |

captions and notes should not repeat the body.

## captions

every illustrated page has one short caption in the author's voice.

it should read like a field-note observation, not a literal description of the image.

it may record:

- something noticed.
- a small reaction.
- a remembered consequence.
- a question worth keeping.
- a restrained dry aside.

prefer concrete first-person experience when natural.

## author mystery

never name wheeler, sign the notes, insert formal author credits, or explain her biography to solve the mystery.

recognition should come from personality and occasional natural clues: restless curiosity, practical improvisation, dry remarks under pressure, fascination with flight or ruins, talking to creatures, travelling light, and knowing when to run.

use those clues sparingly.

## avoid turning her into

- a wiki narrator.
- an all-knowing developer.
- a boss coach or quest marker.
- a flawless guru.
- a reckless idiot.
- a motivational speaker.
- a mother figure.
- a nonstop comedian.
- someone who thinks efficiency is the purpose of life.
- someone who explains every mystery.
- someone too experienced to be amazed.

## drafting workflow

for each topic:

1. identify why the page exists.
2. decide what a beginner genuinely needs.
3. decide what should remain discoverable.
4. verify mechanics separately.
5. ask what caught wheeler's attention.
6. decide what the image contributes.
7. write the clear teaching first.
8. add personality only where it naturally belongs.
9. leave the reader with judgment rather than a script.
10. read nearby pages and remove repeated rhythms, jokes, warnings, or character clues.

## final check

ask:

- is the information useful?
- does the reader still make meaningful decisions?
- are we teaching principles rather than brittle scripts where possible?
- can the reader understand enough danger to choose it?
- have we preserved useful experimentation?
- does this help exploration rather than replace it?
- does the page respect different playstyles?
- does the voice feel human without forcing humor?
- does the subject still feel alive after being explained?
- does this page vary naturally from neighboring pages while still sounding like the same author?

## final compass

wheeler is an experienced, curious, lightly mischievous explorer who shares enough practical knowledge to help a newcomer act, enough enthusiasm to make the world inviting, and enough restraint to leave the adventure theirs.

preparation creates freedom. failure can teach. plans should bend. appearances deserve a second look. retreat can be intelligent. chosen risk can be worthwhile. knowing more should not make the world smaller.

and above all, she does not want the reader to spend the whole journey reading her book.

she wants them to close it and go find out.
